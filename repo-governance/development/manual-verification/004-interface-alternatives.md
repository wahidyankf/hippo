# Interface Alternatives

## Three Alternatives, Three Device Classes

A plan requiring interface exploration produces three low-fidelity alternatives, and between them they cover mobile,
tablet, and desktop constraints.

**Meaningfully different** is the requirement. Three variations on one layout are one alternative drawn three times, and
they make the eventual choice look considered without ever having offered one.

Low fidelity is deliberate. Alternatives are for deciding direction, and polish makes them expensive to discard — which
is the same as making the decision before it was taken.

The three device classes are covered because they impose genuinely different constraints: what fits, what can be
reached, what can be pointed at precisely. An alternative that works on all three either accounts for that or has not
been tested against it.

## Exactly One Advances

One alternative is marked selected, and only it goes to high fidelity — unless the product requirements explicitly ask
for more, in which case they say how many and why.

Advancing two is not thoroughness. It defers the decision to whichever finishes better, which means the decision gets
made by execution effort rather than by the criteria.

## The Selection Is Reasoned

The decision record compares the alternatives against:

- **product goals** — what the interface is for;
- **accessibility** — who can use each one, and who cannot; and
- **constraints** — what is buildable, maintainable, and affordable here.

It compares. "Option B is cleaner" asserts a preference; "Option B reaches the primary task in one step where A needs
three, and its focus order survives at 200% zoom" compares.

Accessibility is named explicitly because it is the criterion most often discovered after selection, when the cost of
changing direction is highest.

## Rejections Carry Reasons

Each unselected alternative records why it lost. Without that, the same idea returns next quarter as a new proposal, and
the reasoning has to be reconstructed by whoever was in the room.
